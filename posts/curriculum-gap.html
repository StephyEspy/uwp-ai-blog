The Curriculum Gap

Many undergraduate CS courses still frame learning as solitary, unaided, and tool-neutral, even as AI systems reshape everyday programming practice.

This article is a placeholder draft. Add specific course examples, instructor perspectives, and researched evidence before final submission.

A curriculum gap appears when students experience one version of computing in class and another version outside it. In class, assignments may be designed around tightly controlled individual work. Outside class, students see AI tools advertised as normal companions for coding, writing documentation, generating tests, and exploring new languages.

Silence becomes instruction

When courses do not address AI tools directly, students still learn something. They learn that the topic is risky, unofficial, or separate from legitimate computer science. That silence can make students less likely to ask careful questions about what responsible use looks like.

Assignments need clearer learning targets

AI tools make it more important to name what an assignment is actually trying to teach. If the goal is syntax practice, outside assistance may undermine the task. If the goal is testing, design reasoning, or code review, AI output can become material for critique.

Closing the gap

Closing the curriculum gap does not require every course to become an AI course. It requires programs to decide where AI belongs, where it should be limited, and how students should explain their choices. That kind of clarity can make academic integrity more teachable instead of only punishable.
