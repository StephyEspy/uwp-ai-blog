AI Preparedness in Computer Science Education

CS programs are still valuable because they teach foundational knowledge, but they are not fully caught up with industry expectations around AI-assisted workflows, code judgment, large codebases, and long-term software engineering practice.

A few years ago, being a "prepared" computer science student mostly meant proving your four years of undergraduate experience gave you the ability to code well enough to secure a respectable junior-level position. You were expected to pledge your brain to learning every relevant data structure and algorithm, understanding the specific syntax and semantics of popular programming languages, surviving operating systems, debugging programs that failed for reasons that felt personally disrespectful, and eventually showing employers that you could turn technical knowledge into working software.

Now, that definition is getting messier.

Generative AI tools are all the rage now: ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, DeepSeek, Microsoft Copilot, Llama, Qwen, and even that list is not exhaustive. Beyond the big-name LLMs, there are AI coding assistants, agents, IDE tools like Cursor, experimental systems, MCP servers, and what virtually feels like a new "revolutionary" product for programmers every other week. For computer science students just trying to keep up, the precarious landscape of AI is far from producing the programmer's predictable, cookie-cutter tech stack as it used to be. Now, you must figure out which tools are worth learning, which are just hype, and how to responsibly use them without outsourcing your own thinking on a whim.

We know these tools are becoming a normal part of how modern software is being written, debugged, reviewed, and explored. But that does not mean AI is making computer science education irrelevant. If anything, AI is exposing what parts of computer science education still matter most, and what parts are not preparing students enough. The issue is not whether students should learn fundamentals or use AI. The issue is whether CS programs are teaching students how to use those fundamentals to judge AI-generated work, navigate large codebases, and make responsible engineering decisions in the kinds of messy software environments they will actually enter.

This engrossment of AI in the technology industry raises an important question that matters a lot for computer science undergraduates: is college actually preparing me for what AI is changing, or am I going to have to figure out these new expectations on my own?

To address that, based on my up-to-date research and personal interview with Justin Jia, a Senior Software Engineer from Apple, the answer to that question is: sort of, but not entirely.

To be more specific, my argument is that computer science programs are still valuable because they establish foundational knowledge students should not dismiss. However, they are not fully caught up with the industry expectation that students know how to integrate AI into engineering workflows, judge AI-generated code, and navigate the messy reality of large codebases that define professional software engineering.

My recommendation, then, is not that universities abandon fundamentals or turn every CS class into an AI tools class. Instead, CS programs should make AI literacy and long-term software judgment more visible across the curriculum, especially through projects where students revise, debug, and maintain code over time rather than only building short assignments from scratch.

This is relevant because this pressure is indisputable for students at this point. Farooqi et al. (2026) found that computer science students are already experiencing job anxiety because AI is changing how students approach learning and completing work within their classes, how they plan for their careers, and their growing wariness over the viability of the entry-level software engineering role. AI is not just changing the expectations for job-assured developers. It is also changing what current computer science students fear about their future before they can even graduate. That matters because students are not just learning technical material anymore; they are also trying to guess which skills will still make them employable by the time they enter the field.

The Curriculum-to-Industry Gap

In my interview with Jia, he raised an important point that often gets overlooked when discussing AI in the context of software engineering and computer science: these two things are related, but not the same thing. In reflecting on his time as a UC Davis undergraduate, he applauded the CS curriculum for laying down the foundational knowledge he carries with himself today, reminiscing that it was "pretty comprehensive" because it gave him the principal knowledge of how a computer system simply works, from hardware-level concepts to programming languages, data structures, and operating systems (Jia, personal communication, 2026).

The foundations of computer science will always be relevant, and arguably, they are what will stick as AI becomes further entrenched into typical engineering workflows. Computer science students need the knowledge and reasoning skills that formal education can offer in order to see through the potential weaknesses and inevitable errors AI tools will produce.

Jia, however, did point out a weakness in the curriculum: he believed that the work students are most commonly assigned within computer science coursework does not really prepare them for the sheer scale, complexity, and intricacies of working as a professional software engineer. This does not mean small-team projects are useless. They teach collaboration, planning, and the experience of building something from the ground up. The problem is that they often stop before students encounter the harder part of software engineering: maintaining, understanding, and safely changing systems they did not fully create themselves.

He draws a comparison between two things: many students will typically encounter homework where they form small teams for a project and learn to build a codebase from the bottom up, as was my experience in many CS courses as a senior now. Once you go industry-side, engineers are usually brought onto large, already existing codebases that have been written, revised, and managed by countless people, across years at times. Jia elaborates on this idea, arguing that genuine software engineering involves "collaborating with a lot of other engineers" and organizing complex systems into smaller, digestible pieces, so your team can divvy up work without needing to memorize the entirety of a codebase (Jia, personal communication, 2026).

This becomes even more important in an AI-heavy workflow because AI tools are usually strongest when the task is small, isolated, and clearly scoped. But professional software engineering is rarely that clean. A generated solution can look correct in one file while quietly breaking assumptions elsewhere in the system. That is why students need more than the ability to produce code; they need practice understanding context, dependencies, and design choices before accepting what AI gives them.

The issue being addressed here is not that undergraduate computer science coursework is useless, but that not much else of significance is really taught to students in preparation for the tech industry. Students need more explicit preparation in how to judge AI-generated code reasonably, how to manage a large codebase and other people's code, and how to understand design choices at a high level that generative AI comprehensively cannot.

Understanding this nuance is important because AI tools, regardless of how well they automate, will not remove the need for old-fashioned software engineering judgment. On the contrary, it makes sound judgment a hot commodity rather than another skill threatened by absorption into AI. AI is fast, everyone knows this, but one of its major pitfalls is its consistent inability to understand long-term design patterns of an actual codebase. It is great for solving small, seemingly isolated problems while simultaneously creating errors elsewhere. Essentially, it is like a driving teacher that excels at showing you how to drive on a straight, open road with zero cars in sight.

Part of the difficulty is that most CS curricula are not designed around one single professional skill. They usually divide knowledge into separate areas: programming, data structures, algorithms, systems, theory, databases, software engineering, and sometimes machine learning or security. AI does not fit neatly into that structure because it can be treated as a tool students use across classes, a topic students study directly, or a disruption that changes how assignments and assessments work.

This also gives an explanation as to why universities are in a tight spot at the moment. Smith (2024) describes AI in computer science education as potentially becoming a well-taught tool or subdomain, which explains why the design of the typical computer science curriculum is struggling to place AI clearly. If AI were to be treated as a tool, for example, there would be courses teaching students how to intelligently prompt an AI model and which AI model is appropriate for certain tasks. If it were treated as a subdomain, it could become a specialized concentration, such as cybersecurity or machine learning, with its own subset of classes for different concerns within AI and AI usage. If it continues to be dealt with as some sort of illusory wildcard, then students will feel an increasing discrepancy between what their job expects they know and what the university is supposed to be preparing them for.

AI Is Not a Replacement Brain

Across the research I have scoped, the major implicit theme I observe from all of the sources I have found is that AI is best positioned when developers and engineers use it as a tool they can collaborate with, not as a machine to pump solutions out of. Shao and Ishengoma (2026) discovered that developers who actively review, accommodate, and confirm the correctness of AI-generated code are currently increasing their productivity more than those who just accept the code without a second thought. What they uncovered suggests that the important skill here is not simply having a plethora of AI tools at your disposal. It is the method by which developers choose to use those tools and knowing how to responsibly integrate AI into a development workflow.

Jia essentially makes the same point as these researchers, with a more practical angle. He gave a well-suited analogy for how AI coding tools should be used, proposing them to be "more like having interns that you can ask for help." They are best when they follow instructions, write syntactically correct code, and are used as an exploration or research tool, but they may not give you the best solution (Jia, personal communication, 2026). That comparison is useful because it does not give in to the hype. Instead, it gives an honest perspective of AI as an intern, someone that is typically helpful, but often susceptible to mistakes like misunderstanding given tasks, not knowing enough context, or producing work that always needs extra review. The same can be said for your use of AI if you dethrone your reasoning for its reasoning.

This is also supported by Brandebusemeyer, Schimmer, and Arnrich (2025), who found that developers' experiences with generative AI are considerably dependent on how they interact with it: whether they use it moderately, rely on it too heavily, review its output carefully, or treat its responses as final answers. Moderate use of AI has been shown to speed up completion time, as well as reduce and mitigate the cognitive load of continuously coding. Yet, overreliance on AI will weaken these upsides. This difference matters especially for students because at face value, the "easier" solution to problem solving in programming is just using generated AI output. In actuality, striking a balance by using AI as an educational tool, using it to quality-check work, and testing and examining its output is a substantially different skill than complete dependency on whatever AI happens to generate in response to a request. The latter is not a reliable practice, or one that teaches good judgment.

This all the more bolsters the idea of AI literacy becoming more ingrained in computer science education. Cerny (2024) argues that AI literacy is not just knowing how to use AI tools, but also the propensity to meaningfully evaluate AI-generated output, understand its limitations, and responsibly take ownership of final decision-making in the process of problem solving. For computer science students, this highly suggests that AI literacy should not be branded as optional knowledge. It should be treated as a direct manifestation of the core principles educators already make a point of in coursework: evaluating, testing, documenting, collaborating, and responsibility.

For students, this means AI literacy should look practical, not abstract. It should include knowing when to ask AI for help, when not to, how to test what it gives back, how to explain why generated code works, and how to reject a solution that looks polished but does not fit the larger system.

The Anxiety Students Feel

While the industry is still in its exploration phase of deciding what the best LLM is or what AI tools an engineer should possess and master in their toolkit, students of computer science and related disciplines are trying their best to gauge what to expect from their future employment. Farooqi et al. (2026) found that post-secondary computer science students are already experiencing job anxiety and uncertainty, in particular around entry-level positions within web development and software engineering. Referencing the same study, students are likewise worried about an "AI takeover" where a near hypothetical future involves the complete obsolescence of the entry-level of software engineering by AI automating the same roles they are preparing for. Students feel an inclination to shift into AI-related coursework, opt for graduate school, or just choose different career paths altogether.

The anxiety students are feeling is completely understandable. If some abstracted technology is taking seconds to generate code with perfect syntax with just a simple prompt, it is not really all that questionable to second guess your career choices, or choice of major for that matter. However, the research I have found nonetheless probes an important question, not one that asks "will my job be taken by AI?" but rather: what parts of software engineering are changing, and how can I become irreplaceable?

The answer my research has been pointing to is in a deep awareness of what it means to judge and evaluate your work. Students should be getting to know how to prompt AI for help, rather than dipping into total abstinence or dependency on it. Students should be learning how to understand and examine its output, detect its bugs, have hindsight knowledge of security risks, and have enough context to understand if a single generated code snippet actually serves as a piece to the puzzle of their work. Cerny (2024) reasons that AI literacy is not simply about using AI in practice; rather, the best process by which to become AI literate is by maintaining a critical evaluation of it and understanding how it directly impacts a developer's decision making. Understanding this is especially crucial for CS students because we have already been taught that developing is not the same as programming. Developing goes beyond just writing code and requires you to think through the design of it.

What Should Universities Do?

The question of how universities should best adapt their curricula to the way software engineering is changing is not yet clear. Smith (2024) explains that computer science educators are still in the process of figuring out how to best incorporate AI into CS education without a loss of its core fundamentals. Some educators are viewing AI from an open perspective, encouraging students to use it to aid in their learning and assignments, while others are viewing it from a more critical lens, pushing for restricting its implementation in the education realm as much as possible. Likely, the majority view centers somewhere in the middle of these two perspectives, all while understanding the long-term impacts of AI to be uncertain, which explains the lack of significant progress from universities in deciding how to best deal with it.

Jia contended a similarly uncertain view on the matter, mentioning that universities "know they have to teach AI," but may not have figured out "the best way to teach AI" yet (Jia, personal communication, 2026). Technology is changing rapidly, and it is not unexpected for universities to be a bit out of date when it comes to adjusting for it. Waiting too long to decide has too many risks; students may graduate college with a foundational understanding of computer science and varying degrees of ability to code well, but all of it is not enough given the growing expectation of AI usage in the kinds of messy, collaborative, long-term projects they will face in industry. What is worse is that the computer science major is losing more and more students each year, with AI being a significant reason students feel less compelled to even choose that route in their undergraduate education.

One possible solution Jia suggested was a longer project-based structure across multiple years, where students continuously build, revise, and evolve a real software system. Instead of only doing ten-week assignments and moving on, students would experience what happens when software grows over time. They could use AI tools, but the project would eventually become complex enough that AI could not simply solve everything for them. That is where the real learning would happen: debugging regressions, simplifying design, responding to users, and realizing that software engineering is not just about making something work once.

For CS students, that may be the real takeaway. AI is not making fundamentals irrelevant. It is exposing whether we actually understand them.
